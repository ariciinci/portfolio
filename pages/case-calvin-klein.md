*Campaign Assets · Production at Volume · Quality Assurance*

![Calvin Klein digital campaigns — assignment overview](images/Ck_Campaigns.jpg)

**Calvin Klein, Amsterdam | June 2022**

I joined Calvin Klein in June 2022 on a short-term freelance assignment, covering for a senior art director during their holiday. The creative and production environment was already established, with existing workflows, templates, production methods, project-management systems and ways of working with the wider creative and marketing teams.

My responsibility was to step into that environment quickly and support the production of digital campaign assets across several activations, working from approved photography, creative direction and campaign materials.

This case study explores how I produced at volume inside an established system, with the 300+ assets delivered for EOSS as the clearest example.

---

<h2 id="the-assignment"> The Assignment: Working Inside an Established Global Creative Environment</h2>

The assignment was to become productive quickly in a global creative environment that already had its own rules. Briefs identified the assets required for each activation, and the supporting materials, existing campaign assets and templates provided the starting point for production.

The work covered **EOSS, CK Jeans Spring, June Refresh, CK Pride Photo Booth and Geofencing Local Pride Moments**. I translated approved campaign material into the digital formats each brief required.

**My focus:** understanding what had already been established, working within it confidently and keeping the work moving through review.

<h2 id="the-role"> The Role: Producing Within an Existing System</h2>

The work started with understanding what had already been established. Before beginning, I made sure I had read the brief and all of the supplied resources carefully. If something wasn't clear, I first investigated it myself rather than immediately passing the question on.

That meant understanding what had already been provided, checking previous or existing assets where relevant, and making sure that any question I raised was specific and actionable. The aim was to arrive at a clear question rather than simply saying that something was unclear.

![Calvin Klein exclusive metallic module — desktop layout](images/CKS_Exclusive_Metallic_Module%203_W_Desktop_Layout.jpg)

**Knowing where to take a question:** not every question needed to go directly to marketing. If the question was creative, I would first discuss it with the creative lead working on the project, such as a senior designer, art director or creative director. If the answer still wasn't clear, I would formulate the question as precisely as possible and align with the producer or project manager.

Questions about the context or scope of the brief were handled through the producer, who managed the traffic between the creative team, marketing and campaign stakeholders. In some situations, the producer would consolidate the questions and take them to marketing rather than creating multiple parallel conversations.

The exception was practical: if a link was broken, a file was missing or something supplied with the brief wasn't working, that could be raised directly. This structure helped keep communication clear and reduced unnecessary noise around the production team.

<h2 id="working-method"> From Brief to Production</h2>

Once the brief and supporting materials were understood, I worked through the requested outputs and checked the relevant requirements. The process was not simply a matter of opening a template and exporting files. I needed to understand:

- what had actually been requested
- which campaign assets were approved
- which existing templates or live assets could be used
- which platform requirements applied
- whether the project information had changed
- what feedback had already been given
- what needed to be produced before the work could move to review

I worked within Calvin Klein's project-management environment in Wrike and used Slack conversations and other project communications to keep up with updates. When information changed, it was important to recognise whether that change affected the work already in progress, the estimated production time or the priority of the assignment.

**Working with the creative lead:** before work moved further into the review process, I made sure the production was complete against the brief and relevant requirements. Where a creative lead was assigned to the project, I showed the work to them first, which gave the creative team an opportunity to review it before it went into the wider review process. I then worked through the feedback, updated the assets and submitted the completed work through the relevant system. That internal creative alignment was an important part of the process.

<h2 id="production-environment"> Production Environment: Templates, Automation & Methods</h2>

Calvin Klein already had a mature digital production setup. I worked with established templates and production methods, including **Adobe XD, Photoshop Actions, automated resizing and exporting**.

The advantage of working this way was consistency and speed, particularly when producing large numbers of related assets. The templates and automation handled repetitive parts of the work, but they didn't remove the need for judgement. Each output still needed to be checked against the brief, the relevant platform requirements and the latest project information.

For me, the important part was learning the system quickly enough to work within it confidently rather than trying to replace it.

<h2 id="eoss"> The Scale: EOSS, 300+ Assets in Two Weeks</h2>

EOSS (End of Season Sale) was the clearest example of production volume during the assignment. During my production period, I personally produced and exported **300+ digital assets in approximately two weeks**.

**300+ assets · ~2 weeks**

![EOSS main landing page — desktop](images/PLACEHOLDER-eoss-main-landing-desktop.jpg)

The work wasn't a single set of layouts. The campaign moved through different stages as the promotion progressed, including further reductions, final reductions, last chance and final hours. Each stage introduced new requirements while the campaign still needed to feel like the same campaign.

The production work had to respond to those changes while maintaining the established visual language. That meant adapting approved campaign material into the formats required at each stage, rather than treating each stage as a completely new piece of creative.

![EOSS campaign progression — Main EOSS, Further Reductions, Final Reductions, Last Chance, Final Hours](images/PLACEHOLDER-eoss-campaign-progression.jpg)

**Production at volume:** the scale of EOSS meant that organisation and prioritisation were important. When several campaign requests were active, I aligned with the producer or project manager on what needed to happen first. I kept them updated on where I was with the current work, what remained to be completed and what was waiting for information, feedback or materials.

This was particularly important when a project was temporarily on hold. Rather than treating everything as equally urgent, the production traffic needed to remain visible so that priorities could be adjusted as information changed. I also gave an indication of how much work remained and what I expected to complete within the day or week, which gave the producer/PM a clearer picture of production capacity.

<h2 id="digital-ecosystem"> The Output: Across the Digital Ecosystem</h2>

The same campaign also needed to work across different placements. I produced and adapted assets for **web, e-commerce, email, paid social and Instagram Stories**, including different desktop, mobile and social formats.

A landing page and an Instagram Story might use the same campaign material, but they don't behave in the same way. The available space, proportions, hierarchy and platform requirements change. The production work was therefore about finding the right adaptation for each output while keeping the campaign recognisable and consistent.

![EOSS cross-channel grid — desktop, mobile, email, Instagram feed, Instagram Story, collection ad](images/PLACEHOLDER-eoss-cross-channel-grid.jpg)

**Staying on top of changing information:** in a live campaign environment, the information you start with isn't necessarily the information you finish with. I used the project-management platform and Slack/Teams channels to stay responsive to the creative team and producer, and kept in regular contact with my creative partner and the wider creative team.

When something changed, I looked at whether it created a conflict with information already received, affected the amount of work required or changed the expected production time. If a change introduced additional work, it was important that this was visible to the producer/PM so that the additional production requirement could be understood and prioritised properly.

![EOSS detail — campaign variation layouts](images/PLACEHOLDER-eoss-detail-variation.jpg)

**Quality assurance:** QA was part of the production process. Before submitting work, I returned to the brief and the information around it and checked the work against what had actually been requested. Depending on the asset, this included checking:

- required formats
- dimensions
- platform requirements
- content and campaign messaging
- file naming
- feedback
- completeness of the requested asset set
- correct destination / recipient
- required sign-off

I also checked relevant Wrike information and Slack conversations when there had been updates during production. The point was not simply to make sure that the design looked finished. The asset needed to be **ready for the next person in the process**.

<h2 id="production-judgement"> The Production Judgement: A Template Issue</h2>

During production, I noticed that some existing social-media templates were following outdated platform requirements. Because the templates were already part of the production system, I didn't change them independently.

I raised the issue with the internal production/creative team and discussed it with the Production Manager. The discussion included whether the templates should be updated and what impact that could have on the production deadline. Once the approach was agreed, I updated the existing templates.

I also retained the previous versions rather than removing them, and communicated the change during a production meeting so the team understood the difference between the previous and updated versions during activation.

It was a relatively small issue, but a useful example of the judgement required in production work: recognising a problem, checking its implications, involving the right people and making a controlled change.

<h2 id="handoff"> The Hand-off: Getting Assets Ready for the Next Stage</h2>

Once the work had gone through the relevant internal review and sign-off, the assets needed to be prepared for the next stage of the campaign. For EOSS, Calvin Klein asked me to provide master assets to its localisation agencies.

My responsibility was to make sure the assets I supplied were correct, followed the brief and had the required sign-off. The localisation itself was handled downstream.

The hand-off therefore looked roughly like:

**Brief** → **Production** → **QA** → **Creative / internal review** → **Sign-off** → **Master asset delivery** → **Downstream localisation / activation**

<h2 id="experience"> The Experience: Working Independently & Professional Contribution</h2>

The team was able to give me work independently because I was used to adapting quickly to new working environments and learning technical platforms and preferred software. I had already worked across different brands and client environments, including adidas and Reebok, and was comfortable entering an established creative structure without needing to be guided through every production decision.

Independence did not mean working in isolation. I understood when I could make a production decision myself, when a creative decision belonged with the creative lead, and when a question about campaign context or scope needed to go through the producer or PM. That distinction helped me work independently without creating unnecessary communication or bypassing the people responsible for the wider campaign.

### What this experience demonstrates

- **Production at volume:** producing and exporting 300+ EOSS assets in approximately two weeks across web, e-commerce, email, paid social and Instagram Stories.
- **Working inside an established system:** learning templates, automation and project-management tools quickly and using them with confidence.
- **Communication and escalation:** knowing whether a question belonged with the creative lead, the producer/PM or marketing, and framing it clearly.
- **Prioritisation and visibility:** keeping the producer/PM informed of progress, capacity and the impact of changing information.
- **Quality assurance:** checking every output against the brief, platform requirements, feedback and sign-off before it moved forward.
- **Production judgement:** recognising an outdated template, involving the right people and making a controlled, documented change.

The common thread is the combination of **production, creative judgement, communication and attention to detail**: producing a high volume of digital assets without losing sight of the details, and making sure each one is ready for the next person in the process.
